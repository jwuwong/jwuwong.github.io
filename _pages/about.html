About

I am a PhD student in the Department of Linguistics at Stanford University. My research sits at the intersection of sociolinguistics; language variation and change; and multilingualism and language contact, with a particular focus on the Chinese diaspora and the study of social meaning.

Dissertation (in progress). My dissertation examines the language of Cantonese immigrant communities in the United States, investigating how sociolinguistic variation, language contact, and immigration shape the construction of local and diasporic identity. Building on my qualifying work in the Greater Boston area, it brings together production and perception to understand how immigrant speakers navigate, signal, and are perceived through their speech.

Much of my work examines the language of Cantonese immigrant communities. My first qualifying paper, Local Accent Meanings: Vocalic Variation of Cantonese Immigrants in the Greater Boston Area, investigates how vocalic variation carries local social meaning, while my second, You're Hearing But Are You Listening: Perception of Category-Divergent Talkers, examines how listeners perceive talkers who diverge from expected categories. I have also worked on intensifier variation and change, code-switching and layered indexicality, and the sociopolitical landscape of multilingual communities such as Boston's Chinatown.

Before coming to Stanford, I earned my M.A. in the Humanities (Linguistics) at the University of Chicago, where my thesis explored the spatial semantics of the Tungusic language family, and my B.A. (Hons) in Linguistics and Visual Arts, also at the University of Chicago, graduating magna cum laude. Along the way I have contributed to research on Arctic Indigenous language contact, the documentation of Washo, sign language linguistics, and the sociophonetics of transgender and transmasculine speakers.
